I’ve spent much of my career working in intelligence, primarily imagery and geospatial analysis. That work taught me to look for patterns, question what I think I know, and recognize how much of an assessment depends on what I can actually observe. It also taught me that understanding what happened is only part of the job. The harder question is what it means and what might happen next.

My current work in Technology Risk & Controls is giving me another perspective on these questions: how an organization manages technology risk and evaluates the controls it depends on. It’s also making me curious about the connection between a control’s intended purpose and the evidence that it works. If an attacker changes their approach, would that control still help? Would we have enough visibility to recognize the change?

I’m now working toward cyber threat intelligence. I bring experience with intelligence analysis, but I’m still building the technical understanding to apply it in this domain. How does an enterprise actually work? Where does an attacker’s activity leave evidence? What can a particular log tell us, and what might we miss if that’s the only place we look?

Those are some of the questions behind this GitHub.

I wanted a place to put that learning into practice and make my thinking visible. Over time, this space will include analysis of public threat reporting, practical investigations, small Python projects, and experiments that connect intelligence questions to technical evidence. Some entries will be finished projects. Others will document a question I’m working through, an assumption that didn’t hold up, or something I had to go back and learn.

One area I keep coming back to is agentic AI and what it could mean for both attackers and defenders over the next few years.

If an attacker can give an agent an objective and let it choose tools, interpret results, and adjust its approach, how much does that change the pace of an intrusion? Which parts still require human expertise? Could agents make familiar techniques easier to carry out at scale, or enable combinations of behavior that we aren’t prepared to recognize?

And if attackers can adapt more quickly, how should intelligence teams adapt with them?

I’m interested in whether we can build tools that connect historical threat reporting to what an analyst is seeing now. Tools that help answer: Where have we seen something similar? What happened next in those cases? What is different about this environment? Which next steps are plausible, and what should we collect to distinguish between them?

That also raises questions about trust. How do we tell the difference between a useful hypothesis and a convincing story? How do we keep an agent from reinforcing our assumptions or treating repeated reporting as independent evidence? What would demonstrate that a tool improves an analyst’s judgment, rather than simply producing more material to review?

I don’t have settled answers to those questions. They’re part of what I want to investigate here.

My aim is to build the technical foundation to explore them seriously, keep the analysis tied to evidence, and document what I learn along the way.
